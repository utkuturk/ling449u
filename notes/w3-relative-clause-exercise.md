# Relative clauses after a preceding production trial

Instructor worksheet for `slides/w3-bock-levelt.qmd`. This is a classroom
extension of the selection questions in Bock and Levelt (1994), sections I and
II. Allow about 15 to 20 minutes for production and discussion.

## Running the activity

The deck runs the heard / listened to demonstration live, one trial per slide,
so the previous sentence is gone before the target appears. Use the separate
cards below only for packets A to D, where different pairs get different
preceding trials. Boxes containing A or B are alternative conditions, not two
successive productions by the same speaker.

1. Practice combining two sentences into a relative clause, retaining the verb
   and event roles. Use the deck's chasing example and one neutral item.
2. Present the **preceding trial**. The speaker combines its two sentences
   aloud. After the response is complete, remove that card.
3. Present the **target trial** on a new card. The speaker combines these new
   sentences accurately and promptly. Nothing from the preceding trial remains
   visible. Keep preparation time and presentation procedure consistent.
4. Record the first target response verbatim, plus repairs. Also note whether
   the preceding response actually contained the intended verb and complement
   pattern. Do not show the error examples or alternative conditions before
   production.
5. Give each pair one packet below, with one student presenting and the other
   speaking. Compare responses across packets afterward. To switch roles,
   use a fresh item set; the presenter has already seen the current target.

We are asking whether having just **used** a verb or complement pattern affects
later production. Students should try to speak correctly. The prediction could
concern verb reuse, preposition realization, or fluency. Errors are possible
outcomes, not something participants should intentionally produce.

These few trials are a demonstration, not a test with enough observations to
establish an effect. If everyone answers correctly, discuss the hypothetical
responses. The transformation also involves reading and source memory, so a
verb substitution cannot automatically be localized to lemma selection.

## Demonstration: heard, then listened to

Present these on separate cards, in this order.

**Preceding trial**

- I remember the musician.
- Maya heard the musician.
- Begin: **I remember the musician that ...**

Expected: *I remember the musician that Maya heard.*

**Target trial**

- I met the singer.
- Katie listened to the singer.
- Begin: **I met the singer that ...**

Expected: *I met the singer that Katie listened to.*

For another group, replace the preceding second sentence with *Maya spoke to
the musician*. Keep the target identical. This provides a different complement
pattern, but the two preceding verbs also differ in meaning. Use that limitation
to motivate the four conditions below. Hearing and listening themselves differ
in intentionality; see [Cambridge's comparison](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/hear-or-listen-to).

## Packets A to D: change what was produced previously

Assign one packet to each pair. Each packet contains a preceding card and a
separate target card. Show only the assigned version, not this comparison table.

For **every preceding card**, the first sentence is *I saved the invitation*.
The second sentence varies:

| Packet | Second sentence of preceding trial | Intended comparison |
|---|---|---|
| A | Alex answered the invitation. | Similar response meaning, NP pattern |
| B | Alex responded to the invitation. | Similar response meaning, PP with to |
| C | Alex deleted the invitation. | Less similar meaning, NP pattern |
| D | Alex referred to the invitation. | Less similar meaning, PP with to |

Begin each preceding response: **I saved the invitation that ...**

After that response, remove the card. The **target card is identical** for all
four packets:

- I opened the e-mail.
- Katie replied to the e-mail.
- Begin: **I opened the e-mail that ...**

### Answer key

| Packet | Preceding response |
|---|---|
| A | I saved the invitation that Alex answered. |
| B | I saved the invitation that Alex responded to. |
| C | I saved the invitation that Alex deleted. |
| D | I saved the invitation that Alex referred to. |

Target in every packet: *I opened the e-mail that Katie replied to.*

The different people and objects help distinguish the preceding event from the
new event. Both trials require the same type of relative clause. The conditions
are candidate materials, not a matched stimulus set: verb meaning, frequency,
length, naturalness, and form similarity need checking across many items.

## Record what was produced

| Packet | Actual preceding response | First target response | Produced verb | Preposition | Repair / other change |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | | |

If the preceding response differs from its intended form, retain both the
assigned condition and actual production. In a larger experiment, decide in
advance how to handle those trials; silently reassigning them can bias a
comparison.

For a *replied to* target after *answered*:

| Target ending produced | Observation |
|---|---|
| replied to | Target retained |
| replied | Target verb retained, required preposition missing |
| answered | Earlier verb reused, compatible complement pattern |
| answered to | Earlier verb reused, incompatible preposition in the intended response sense |

For the *listened to* demonstration, the corresponding endings are *listened
to*, *listened*, *heard*, and *heard to*.

Record other verbs, different prepositions, repeated noun phrases, participant
changes, unfinished responses, and repairs separately. Assess compatibility
with the produced verb in the intended sense and the speaker's variety.
A grammatical alternative such as *the singer to whom Katie listened* is a
format deviation, not a preposition omission. See [Cambridge's relative-pronoun
account](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/relative).

## What to compare

Ask these questions separately:

- Is the **earlier verb reused** more often after a related preceding sentence?
- When the **target verb is retained**, is its preposition missing more often
  after an NP pattern than after a PP pattern?
- Does the same contrast appear for the less-related preceding verbs?
- Are there repairs or hesitations even when the final sentence is correct?

For one simple tally, suppose eight responses retain *listened* and two of
those omit *to*. The omission count is **two out of eight**. Responses that
switch to *heard* contribute to the verb-substitution count; their lack of *to*
is compatible with the produced verb. This is a hypothetical example, not
class data. Record the denominator in each condition, since the number of
retained target verbs can differ.

## Discussion prompts and possible interpretations

**Only the earlier verb is reused.** This suggests an effect on lexical
fidelity to the source. It does not establish a specific selection mechanism;
source-memory confusion is another possibility.

**The target verb survives but its preposition is missing more often after an
NP pattern.** Reuse of an earlier complement pattern is one candidate account.
Failure during later realization is another. The observed omission does not
by itself distinguish them.

**A preceding PP supports correct to on the next trial.** This could involve
reuse of a complement pattern or repetition of the word *to*. Comparing related
and less-related PP verbs tests whether semantic similarity matters, but does
not fully separate those two explanations.

**No errors occur.** Use the response examples to formulate predictions. A few
correct responses do not show that earlier production has no effect. Reliable
claims about timing would require appropriate measurement and more trials.

Ask each group to propose one additional comparison:

1. Keep the target fixed and change the preceding sentence's meaning while
   holding its complement pattern similar.
2. Insert one unrelated filler trial between preceding and target trials to
   test whether any effect changes with intervening production. Do not insert
   fillers for only one complement condition.
3. Compare a preceding sentence with *to* in another construction, to investigate
   repetition of the word versus reuse of the relevant complement pattern.
4. Use simple-sentence targets with the same verbs to assess what the relative
   clause contributes.

For an experiment, rotate conditions across items and speakers, so that each
speaker sees a target only once. Include neutral preceding sentences using the
same production task, and fillers between complete preceding-target pairs to
reduce predictability. Keep the within-pair interval consistent unless it is
the variable being tested. Pilot the materials and acceptability judgments.

## Candidate verb families

Use these as stimulus-design prompts in the senses specified, not as a list of
universal grammaticality judgments.

| NP pattern | PP pattern | Context to establish |
|---|---|---|
| hear the singer | listen to the singer | perception versus deliberate attention |
| answer the e-mail | reply/respond to the e-mail | responding to a message |
| watch the birds | look at the birds | visual attention |
| deride the proposal | laugh at the proposal | ridicule rather than amusement alone |
| seek a solution | look for a solution | searching |
| request an extension | ask for an extension | requesting something |
| obey the rules | abide by the rules | compliance |
| follow the protocol | adhere to the protocol | compliance |
| follow the regulations | comply with the regulations | compliance |
| invoke Zeus | call upon Zeus | an appeal, not merely calling someone's name |
| obey Elizabeth | answer to Elizabeth | authority, not answering a question |
| trust Chelsea | confide in Chelsea | related but different interpersonal acts |

For a direct test of **which** preposition is produced, compare *adhere to*
with *comply with*. Both use a PP. Score *adhered with* and *complied to*
separately from omission. Have speakers use one verb in the preceding trial
and the other in the target trial. This lets us ask about preposition identity
as well as whether a preposition is present.

Do not automatically mark *trust in Chelsea* as ungrammatical: *trust in*
has a use with people as well as things. The contrast with *confide in* therefore
needs a carefully specified sense and speaker judgments.
[Oxford's trust in entry](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/american_english/trust-in).
Likewise, an acceptable sentence with a changed interpretation should be coded
as a meaning change, not simply a syntactic failure. The listed pairs differ
in semantic equivalence, register, and available argument patterns.

## Separate case and dative questions

The object relative *the singer that Katie listened to* contains no overt
dependent pronoun whose case form could be scored. A separate subject-relative
probe can retain one:

- Input: *I met the singer. The singer listened to her.*
- Target: *I met the singer that listened to her.*
- Hypothetical outputs: *listened to she* (pronoun form), *listened her*
  (preposition omission), *listened she* (both observed changes).

A possible preceding production is *I met the drummer that heard her*. Both
trials use subject relatives and retain an overt object pronoun. This is a
separate probe from the earlier object-relative activity, and it is no longer
in the deck.

Both *hear her* and *listen to her* use the same pronoun form. To study
competition between case values, add a manipulation of grammatical role or
use a language with a relevant overt case contrast. Changing relative-clause
type also changes planning demands, so compare like constructions within
this probe.

For the dative question, distinguish *give Chelsea an explanation / give an
explanation to Chelsea* from *explain the problem to Chelsea*. The ordinary
two-object counterpart with *explain* is not licensed in the illustrated
variety. [Cambridge on objects](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/direct-objects),
[Cambridge on explain](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/explain).

Target RC: *the problem that Katie explained to Chelsea*. The hypothetical
*the problem that Katie explained Chelsea* could reflect a different frame or
simple omission of *to*. A simple-sentence control reveals more:

- *explained the problem Chelsea*: missing preposition, original noun order.
- *explained Chelsea the problem*: recipient before theme as well as no *to*.

This supplies additional observations about order. It still does not prove
which internal process failed. Do not count grammatical dative alternatives
with verbs such as *give* as errors solely because the surface pattern changed.

The deck's proposed preceding-trial comparison uses these two alternatives:

- A: *I remember the explanation. Alex gave Jordan an explanation.*
  Produced: *I remember the explanation that Alex gave Jordan.*
- B: *I remember the explanation. Alex gave an explanation to Jordan.*
  Produced: *I remember the explanation that Alex gave to Jordan.*

Both groups then receive *I understood the problem. Katie explained the problem
to Chelsea*. Target: *I understood the problem that Katie explained to Chelsea*.
